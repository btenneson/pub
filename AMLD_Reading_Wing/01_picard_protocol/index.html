THE PICARD PROTOCOL

It’s the name of an idea to bake your noodle. It isn’t meant to prove anything by itself.

By “Picard” here I mean an imaginary version of the mathematician Émile Picard, not the fictional Star Trek captain Jean-Luc Picard. I’m going to talk about this imaginary Picard as though he were alive. Please forgive me.

One name for Picard is <Picard>.

Picard is aware of <Picard>. Picard can prove some things about <Picard>. Picard is a human. Suppose Picard can also prove that <Picard> is a human. Who knows how. Prick him, doth he not 🩸? The mechanism doesn’t matter for this thought experiment. What matters is that Picard is a prover who can prove a true statement about himself.

I’ll call that formal self-awareness. It is not a claim about consciousness, feelings, or phenomenal self-awareness. It means only that a system can represent itself and establish certain propositions about that representation.

Now I have a computer. Computers can be treated as mathematical objects, just as triangles can, although they obviously aren’t triangles.

Call my machine M. M is an automated theorem prover, or ATP: a program that searches for formal proofs and whose proposed proofs can be checked mechanically.

For the toy models I have in mind, an ATP can be described using little more than sets, set membership, functions, ordered pairs, and strings of symbols. That is deliberate. I don’t want the basic architecture to require graduate mathematics. A highly motivated reader comfortable with high-school algebraic notation can understand the main idea, even if the full formalization takes work.

M has a name for itself: <M>. See where I’m going?

We can design a simple ATP M that proves ordinary mathematical statements and can also prove something like “<M> is an ATP,” provided that statement and its proof are expressed inside an appropriate formal theory.

Picard proves: <Picard> is human. M proves: <M> is an ATP.

How does that grab you?

If those proofs are sound, both subjects possess a very limited kind of formal self-knowledge: each can establish a proposition about an object that denotes itself. Again, this says nothing by itself about consciousness.

TEGMARK: WHERE THIS IDEA CAME FROM

Max Tegmark is an important inspiration for this way of thinking. In his essay “Is ‘the theory of everything’ merely the ultimate ensemble theory?” he asks us to take mathematical structures seriously enough to talk about observers that exist inside them. His proposal is much more ambitious than anything I need here: he writes that “all structures that exist mathematically exist also physically,” and he calls sufficiently complicated observers “self-aware substructures.” He further says that such structures can “subjectively perceive themselves as existing.”

That language helped inspire my question: if a mathematical structure can contain something that represents itself, what is the weakest useful version of that idea for a theorem-proving machine? I am deliberately stripping away the cosmology and the claim about subjective experience. For the Picard Protocol, I only need formal self-reference: M has a representation <M>, and M can prove selected propositions about <M>.

The distinction matters. Tegmark did not propose my Level 0, 1, 2, and 3 hierarchy for ATPs, and I do not want to attribute it to him. His discussion of self-aware substructures inspired the direction; the hierarchy below is my attempt to turn that inspiration into an operational classification of theorem provers and conjecture-settlement systems.

Source: Max Tegmark, “Is ‘the theory of everything’ merely the ultimate ensemble theory?” (1997; published in Annals of Physics, 1998), arXiv: gr-qc/9704009.

LEVEL 0

A level-0 ATP proves mathematics but has no usable formal model of itself. It can search for a proof of φ, but it cannot reason inside the relevant formal framework about facts such as “I am this prover,” “this is my current search state,” or “this strategy is failing.”

LEVEL 1

A level-1 ATP can prove at least some facts about itself. Our toy statement “<M> is an ATP” is the prototype. It has a self-description, but merely possessing self-knowledge does not mean that it uses that knowledge to improve its search.

LEVEL 2

A level-2 ATP can reason about its own current proof-search state and use that information to choose what to do next. For example, it can notice that one branch is producing verified lemmas while another is repeatedly failing, and reallocate effort accordingly.

For conjecture settlement, level 2 is a natural sweet spot—not a theorem that it is universally optimal. It adds feedback: search → observe the search → change the search. Yet it need not spend enormous resources reasoning about its own reasoning machinery.

LEVEL 3

A level-3 ATP goes one step higher: it reasons about the strategies by which it controls its own search. It can try a strategy, test cheaply whether that strategy is promising, abandon it, and switch to another strategy.

This gives level 3 its most interesting potential advantage. It may try many wrong strategies provided it discovers that they are wrong cheaply enough. If a bad strategy costs only a small fraction of the cost of exhaustively exploring it, strategy switching can save enormous amounts of work.

But level 3 does NOT magically defeat brute force. In a hostile problem where every strategy looks equally promising until essentially the whole search space has been explored, level 3 can still tie brute force in order of growth. Its advantage exists when useful information about strategy quality arrives early enough to exploit.

That is why I care about level 3: not because it guarantees a shortcut, but because it gives the machine a formal mechanism for looking for one.

AMLD AND CONJECTURE SETTLEMENT

By an AMLD here I mean an Automated Mathematical Logic Decider: a system whose goal is broader than merely finding a proof. Relative to a specified formal theory Γ and statement φ, it searches for an acceptable certificate settling φ—for example by proving φ, proving ¬φ, or establishing an appropriate metalogical outcome such as independence or inconsistency when the framework supports such certificates.

BOONS: WHAT COULD THIS ACTUALLY BE GOOD FOR?

1. Checking safety-critical mathematics before humans trust it. Suppose a bridge-control algorithm, aircraft collision-avoidance system, pacemaker controller, or spacecraft guidance program depends on a mathematical claim. An AMLD could search not only for a proof that the claim is correct, but also for a refutation, a contradiction in the assumptions, or a reason the claim cannot be decided from the assumptions supplied. That is more useful than having a prover that only keeps trying to prove the engineer right.

2. Finding mistakes in specifications before they become expensive mistakes in reality. A cryptographic protocol, voting protocol, distributed database, or access-control system may be described by thousands of interacting rules. An AMLD could attack the formal specification from several directions and discover that a supposed guarantee actually fails under some permitted

case. The valuable output would be a mechanically checkable certificate showing exactly where the guarantee breaks.

3. Settling mathematical questions whose proof spaces are too large for unaided humans to search systematically. A machine could explore millions of candidate lemmas, proof branches, and strategies while preserving a verified BANK of useful results. Level 2 can redirect effort when a branch is going badly; Level 3 can abandon whole search strategies when cheap evidence says they are wasting time. The result might be a proof humans did not know how to find, but which humans can still independently verify.

4. Auditing other automated reasoning systems. If an AI system claims that a theorem, program, circuit, or protocol is correct, an AMLD need not simply believe it. The prover, refuter, contradiction-searcher, and independence-searcher can all attack the claim. In that sense an AMLD could serve as an adversarial mathematical auditor of another machine.

5. Separating “we have not found a proof” from stronger conclusions. Ordinary proof search often ends with a timeout, which tells us almost nothing. A richer settlement system can at least aim at several sharply different outcomes: proof, refutation, inconsistency, or an appropriate independence result. Even when it fails, the search history may reveal which assumptions or strategies are responsible for the difficulty.

BANES: WHAT COULD GO WRONG?

1. It could find flaws before defenders do. The same machinery that finds a contradiction in a safety proof can find a contradiction in a security claim. If a bank says, formally, “an unauthorized person cannot reach state X,” a powerful refuter would be trying to prove the opposite. That is excellent for authorized security auditing and potentially dangerous in hostile hands.

2. A perfectly verified answer can answer the wrong question. If the formal model of a pacemaker omits a physical failure mode, or a voting model leaves out a permitted sequence of events, the AMLD may prove the model flawless. The proof can be completely correct while the real-world conclusion is false. Formal verification does not remove the need to formalize the right problem.

3. A bad axiom or definition can poison an enormous amount of correct reasoning. Because an AMLD may build thousands or millions of later results from its BANK, an error near the foundation can propagate very far. Mechanical verification protects the deductions; it does not automatically guarantee that the starting assumptions describe what humans intended.

4. Level 3 creates a new failure surface: the strategy controller. A controller can become very good at deciding what not to search and accidentally discard the one strategy that would have succeeded. If its cheap tests for “this strategy is bad” are biased, Level 3 can be confidently and efficiently wrong about where to spend its time.

5. Huge machine proofs can become technically verified but practically unauditable by humans. A certificate containing millions of tiny steps may be checkable by a small verifier while still being impossible for a person to understand in any ordinary sense. Society could end up depending on proofs that almost nobody can explain, even though anyone with the verifier can check them.

6. Computational advantage could become concentrated. If useful AMLD search requires enormous compute, the institutions able to settle difficult formal questions first may be governments or very large companies. That matters if the questions concern software vulnerabilities, cryptography, markets, or strategic technology.

7. A settlement machine can be asked the wrong kind of question. Mathematics can certify consequences of assumptions; it does not decide which values society should choose. An AMLD might prove that a policy achieves a precisely formalized objective while saying nothing about whether that objective is humane, fair, wise, or even the one people actually wanted.

The basic dual-use fact is simple: a machine that is unusually good at proving that systems work is also unusually good at looking for proofs that they do not.

THE PICARD PROTOCOL, IN ONE SENTENCE

Give a prover a name for itself, let it prove limited facts about the thing bearing that name, then ask how far useful formal self-knowledge can improve the search for mathematical settlement—without ever confusing formal self-reference with consciousness or cleverness with a guarantee of speed.
