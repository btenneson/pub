Depths of a Possible Machine

Semi-Ideal Computers, Certified Transformations, and the Geometry of Potential Mathematical Intelligence

Research direction: Brian Tenneson
Mathematical drafting and analysis: ChatGPT (Picard research protocol)

Extending the formal questions of Depths of a Simulation, this paper separates present deductive competence, successors a machine could attain, and revisions it can actually discover. It studies costed revision graphs, exact finite settlement profiles, arbitrary-depth revision hierarchies, simulation incomparability, joint-workload limits, black-box discovery lower bounds, and preservation requirements for a generalized Picard protocol. A final section interprets legal revisions as sovereign gifts.

Research status: 15-page research edition 001. Conditional theorems and finite regression checks; not a live self-improving machine or a claim that formal self-awareness entails consciousness.

Who and what is Picard?

Picard is a named mathematical research and writing role operated through ChatGPT, not a separate human author or an independently deployed AI system. The work is directed by Brian Tenneson, Research Director. In this collaboration, Tenneson defines the scientific questions, contributes the underlying research manuscripts and mathematical frameworks, and sets the scope. ChatGPT working under the Picard research protocol develops and tests definitions, explores mathematical constructions, proposes lemmas and proofs, seeks counterexamples and obstruction certificates, connects proposals to prior literature, and prepares draft manuscripts for audit and revision.

The name Picard also refers to an earlier formal Picard Protocol in Tenneson's semi-ideal-computer (SIC) research, concerning a mathematical machine's ability to prove a specified property of its recoded copy. That formal protocol is a research object; calling the current ChatGPT collaboration Picard does not establish that it satisfies the idealized protocol, has consciousness, possesses autonomous authority, runs continuously, or has installed self-modifying code.

Picard's editorial distinction is between mathematical possibility and demonstrated capability: a manuscript-level argument is not the same thing as an independently checked proof; a finite test is not a general theorem; a proposed verifier or successor contract is not an operational deployment. These two articles explicitly preserve those distinctions, credit established results, and expose remaining research obligations.

                                                   Abstract
         A machine’s deductive state does not determine the capabilities of its possible successors. Starting
     from the semi-ideal computer (SIC), clocked simulation, recoding and formal reflection of Depths
     of a Simulation, we distinguish present competence, attainable capability and discoverable revision.
     A revision-budgeted settlement profile admits exact finite computation, saturation and optimality
     certificates. We establish arbitrarily deep revision hierarchies, characterize the attainable shapes
     of finite potential profiles, and construct a conservative successor whose target-stage performance
     improves while remaining incomparable under the original clocked simulation preorder. Single-
     target potential fails to determine joint workload capability. A black-box discovery bound separates
     inexpensive possible revisions from inexpensive discoveries. A generalized Picard protocol binds
     preservation, lawful installation and improvement evidence to the parent. The final specialization
     interprets revisions as sovereign gifts in the 24th century. Established mathematical machinery is
     credited; the paper is a conditional mathematical study with finite regression checks, not a deployed
     self-improving machine.

Status. Standalone research companion, not a replacement for the July 27 merged edition or its
November 9 audit. All general results are restricted to their stated mathematical hypotheses. No
new authority, live agent, or runtime installation is asserted. The term “24th century” denotes an
underlying sovereign game, not a chronological prediction. Reading map. Sections 1–3 inherit the SIC
foundations and define complete revision-capable configurations. Sections 4–6 develop potential profiles,
simulation incomparability, and workload comparisons. Sections 7–9 study discovery, the generalized
Picard protocol and sovereign specialization. Sections 10–12 place the results in the literature, report
the audit and identify open obligations.

1     Inheritance from the original theory
The original document [1] asks what constitutes a computer, how computers can simulate one another,
and what a machine can prove about itself. Its Definition 1.2 fixes a formal system F = (x, y, z):
alphabet, well-formed finite strings, and finitary partial rules. Its Definition 2.3 fixes a SIC M = (C, H)
over Y ⊆ y. For Γ ⊆ Y and m ∈ N+ , C(Γ, m) is a subset of Y , and H(Γ, m) is a bit. The initial state is
C(Γ, 1) = Γ; formula states grow monotonically; halting persists and freezes the state. A target-search
ATP for ϕ obeys the sound stopping condition H = 1 ⇒ ϕ ∈ C and eventual halting whenever Γ ⊢F ϕ.
Deductive soundness of its deposits is an additional obligation, for example by rule-driven growth
(original Proposition 2.15).
   The original Definition 3.15 defines M ⪯clk N by a state injection f : SM → SN and an admissible
clock ρ with ρ(1) = 1, nondecreasing and unbounded, satisfying, for all input states p and all m,

                   f (CM (p, m)) = CN (f (p), ρ(m)),       HM (p, m) = HN (f (p), ρ(m)).

This is stronger than a comparison of target success or cost. The original Proposition 3.4 makes the
set-theoretic state space SM = P(Y ); Remark 3.25 explicitly warns that coding every such state is
impossible for infinite countable Y . We therefore use finite states or specified countable effectively
presented configurations whenever claiming an algorithm.
   Original Theorem 4.8 transports proofs through invertible recoding and preserves shortest proof
length. Definition 4.19 (the Picard Protocol) asks a rule-driven target-search ATP to establish a true
assertion that its recoded copy is an ATP, without that target being among its initial hypotheses.
Theorem 4.21 exhibits a minimal witness. Part II treats formal self-awareness as a tagged provability
structure; Definition 15.17 grades the reflection sequence, and Theorems 20.4 and 21.1 address an
idealized mathematician and a reflection-closure machine respectively. Theorem 21.6 distinguishes the
competence to establish arbitrarily deep finite reflection from the performance of actually doing so.
None of those facts amounts to a claim about consciousness.
   Our question is a successor of those questions: what transformations are accessible to a machine,
what can it establish about those transformations, and what can it actually accomplish after paying for
them? This question does not require a sovereign player in every definition. The authorized-revision
interpretation in Section 9 comes last.

1.1   An inherited boundary worth repairing
Original Definition 2.11 uses the non-frozen shortest-proof-layer function for a target-search SIC. If a
target first appears at one stage and a different formula first appears later, that definition can violate
the freeze-on-halt axiom of Definition 2.3. For example, with p → q → r as unary rules, initial {p} and
target q, the shortest proof lengths of q, r are two and three. Freezing the target version at m∗ = ∥q∥F (Γ)
repairs this: set C(Γ, m) = C bf (Γ, min(m, m∗ )), with min(m, ∞) = m. The first-halting-stage identity
of Theorem 2.12 survives. This is a local correction proposal to the supplied text, not a claim that its
original PDF has been edited. Even a perfectly defined shortest-proof-layer stage is not an executable
wall-clock or primitive-operation bound; the 2026 ATLAS amendments already emphasize the distinction
between size and search time.

Definition 2.2 (Revision-capable configuration). A configuration is a record s = (B, K, A, L, u) of

individual receipts need not halt the machine; only the declared terminal condition stops it.

Theorem 2.4 (Conditional inheritance). Suppose U0 is consistent and each committed increment
carries sound evidence of consistency of Ui−1 ∪ ∆i and of the actual operational preservation obligations.
Suppose ordinary and revision events preserve the validity and scope of every retained proof record, and
every new object receipt is accepted by the unchanged sound V. Then every finite committed prefix has

merely from revision.

Proposition 3.2 (Knowledge–capability separation). Even for one fixed formal system, target and
sound checker, equal initial formula knowledge and equal deductive closure do not determine J0 .

4     The geometry of potential development
Theorem 4.1 (Layered potential and finite saturation). Let X be finite, |X| = n ≥ 1, with a complete
finite edge list and nonnegative rational costs. Then:

    1. Jk+1 (s, G) ≤ Jk (s, G) for every k;

    2. Jk equals ordinary shortest-path cost in the graph of states (x, i), 0 ≤ i ≤ k, with edges (x, i) →
       (y, i + r(e)) whenever e : x → y and the endpoint index is at most k, starting at (s, 0) and targeting
       G × {0, . . . , k};

    3. J∞ = Jn−1 , and every finite Jk has a witnessing path of at most n − 1 edges.

Proof. Increasing k only enlarges the path set. Each legal path of revision count at most k lifts uniquely
from (s, 0) by summing revision indicators; projecting a layered path yields the corresponding legal path
with equal charge. For the last assertion, delete any closed segment between repeated complete states

Page 5

of a finite path. Nonnegative cost and nonnegative revision count ensure neither resource increases.
Legal continuation is preserved because the repeated state includes all relevant history, authority and
budget information. A simple path remains, with at most n − 1 edges. There are finitely many such
paths in a finite multigraph. A finite infimum is therefore attained and no revision count above n − 1
improves it.

Algorithm. Build the layered graph and run a nonnegative shortest-path algorithm (e.g. Dijkstra
[19]). There are (k + 1)n vertices and at most (k + 1)|E| edges, with k capped at n − 1 by the theorem.
A binary-heap implementation costs O((k + 1)(n + |E|) log((k + 1)n)) arithmetic/queue operations
under the usual indexed-heap implementation; exact rational arithmetic and input construction costs
are additional. The finite graph itself may be exponentially large or impossible to enumerate in an
unrestricted ATP. No complexity claim is made about synthesizing this graph.

Theorem 4.2 (Strict revision-depth hierarchy). For every positive integer d there exists a finite
revision-capable configuration with one fixed sound proof task and unchanged object-theory deductive
closure such that
                              J0 = J1 = · · · = Jd−1 = ∞,    Jd = d + 1.

Proof. Fix the true hypothesis p and a valid unary formal inference p ⇒ t (e.g. t = p ∨ p). The
controller has states s0 , . . . , sd , each carrying p and the same proof theory. The only non-idle transitions
before sd are the successive authorized revisions si → si+1 , each unit cost, with distinct version-indexed
controller facts. At sd an ordinary proof-and-receipt action of cost one becomes enabled and leads to
g. No earlier state has that action. A finite controller table with disjoint version-indexed facts is a
model of its stipulated controller specification. Each legal route to g crosses every revision edge, so at
least d revisions and one ordinary action are necessary. The chain itself attains that charge. All object
consequences remain unchanged. This establishes a family of abstract finite controller models, not the
admissibility of every chain in a particular gift catalogue.

The word “depth” has three different meanings here: original proof length, original reflection rank, and
the number of legally committed revisions required to enable a settlement. Theorem 4.2 separates the
third from the first two. It does not say that acquiring higher depth is desirable.

4.1    Certificates rather than merely computed scores
Theorem 4.4 (Potential certificates). In the finite layered graph, a function h with nonnegative extended
rational values, h = 0 at all goal nodes, and

                              h(v) ≤ w(v, u) + h(u)       for every edge v → u

certifies a lower bound on remaining shortest-path charge. A legal path with charge h(v) < ∞ certifies
equality. A set D containing the start, disjoint from the goals, and closed under all outgoing layered edges
certifies impossibility. Each finite instance admits an exact certificate of optimality or impossibility.
Proof. Iterate the displayed inequality along any finite goal path. Its last value is zero, so h(v) is at
most the path charge. A matching path gives equality. If D is closed, induction on path length shows
no path from D reaches a goal. For existence of certificates, let h be actual shortest-path distance to
the goals, using ∞ for unreachable nodes; its inequalities follow by concatenation. A finite distance is
attained by Theorem 4.1 applied to the layered graph, or by ordinary cycle deletion. The non-reaching
nodes form a closed set. All comparisons are local checks on an explicitly complete finite graph.

  This is the standard shortest-path potential principle in a revision-budgeted state space; it connects
directly to Where Infinity Breaks and the proof-cut work [5, 6]. A lower bound on a restricted candidate
graph is not necessarily a lower bound on the full graph. An externally checked abstraction must relax
every relevant concrete transition at no greater cost and preserve goal observations in the appropriate
direction. Without that coverage, a negative conclusion is restricted to the supplied graph.


5     Revision reachability is not simulation
Define s ⪯rev t when a finite legal path using only revision edges leads from s to t; include the empty
path. If proposal/administrative events are required, use complete authorized revision transactions as
edges or define a separately named mixed reachability relation. With states that include full history,
reachability is reflexive and transitive by concatenation; its mutual-reachability equivalence yields
the usual partial order on strongly connected classes. If each commit strictly extends an immutable
ledger, there are no nonempty revision cycles, and the relation is already antisymmetric on complete
configurations. A rollback of active policy need not return to the old complete state. Forgetting the
ledger can create apparent cycles.
  Define s ⪯pot t when Jk (t, Ga ) ≤ Jk (s, Ga ) for every specified a, k in a common observation contract.
This is a preorder by numerical transitivity. Neither relation forces the other: an authorized change may
cost more or remove useful actions; two machines with equal or ordered potential need not be linked by
a permitted revision. They also need not admit a clocked simulation, as the next construction proves.

5.1   A conservative faster successor incomparable under clocked simulation
Theorem 5.1 (Clocked incomparability with strict target-stage improvement). There are two finite,
sound target-search SICs M, N for the same base formal theory and target such that N halts no later
than M on every input and strictly earlier on one, but neither M ⪯clk N nor N ⪯clk M in the original
Definition 3.15. The successor’s shortcut is deductively conservative.
Proof. Let Y = {p, q, r} with unary rules p ⇒ q and q ⇒ r, and target r. Treat the formulas as strings
and the rules as the stipulated formal system. If desired, interpret q = p ∨ p and r = q ∨ q in a sound
propositional calculus. At stage one both machines expose the input A ⊆ Y . If r ∈ A, halt and freeze.
Otherwise M adjoins r if q ∈ A, adjoins q if p ∈ A but q ∈  / A, and does nothing otherwise. Repeat until
halt. N adjoins r whenever p or q is present, using the certified derived rule p ⇒ r when necessary; it
exposes the final conclusion, not the macro’s intermediate q. The expanded proof remains available as
provenance. Both are monotone in stage, freeze on halt, and are target-search ATPs on all eight inputs.
N is rule-driven in the conservative extended presentation, not claimed rule-driven for the original
primitive rule list.
   For input {p}, M has three distinct states {p}, {p, q}, {p, q, r}; every trajectory of N has at most two.
An injective state map makes simulation of M into N impossible, regardless of admissible clock.
   In the other direction both state spaces equal P(Y ), so the injection is a bijection. It preserves initial
halting, and because the clock is unbounded it preserves eventual halting in both directions. Hence
it maps the three initially nonhalting but eventually halting inputs {p}, {q}, {p, q} bijectively onto
themselves. The three inputs of N end at three distinct terminal states, whereas those of M end at
only two distinct terminal states. Taking a sufficiently late common stage contradicts injection. Finally,
halting stages are 3 versus 2 on {p}; both are 2 on {q}, {p, q}, both 1 on any input already containing
r, and both infinite on the empty input. The derived rule expands to two legal primitive steps; it adds
no theoremhood.

                   Input    M stage / N stage      M terminal state     N terminal state
                    {p}             3/2            {p, q, r}            {p, r}
                    {q}             2/2            {q, r}               {q, r}
                   {p, q}           2/2            {p, q, r}            {p, q, r}

The comparison is deliberately at the original SIC exposed-formula state and halting-stage level. It
does not show that an implementation with full internal macro states cannot simulate M , nor that a
one-stage macro saves primitive work. A revision granting this macro could preserve the old policy as
an optional alternative in its complete controller. The two fixed-policy SIC snapshots would still have
the incomparability just proved. This is a concrete reason not to use the clocked preorder alone as the
improvement criterion.


6     Potential is richer than a scalar intelligence score
Theorem 6.1 (Single-target potential is not portfolio potential). There are two finite legal configurations
having identical initial knowledge and identical Jk for every singleton target and every revision budget,
but different cost to achieve both targets in one continuing execution.
Proof. Let a, b be two consequences of a common fixed seed, and let their receipts persist once issued.
In system X, the controller may issue a or b at unit cost, but an irreversible authorization choice
prevents issuing the other. In system Y , either may be issued at unit cost and then the other at unit
cost. Use no revision edges. Both have singleton horizons one for each target, independent of k. For
the joint goal requiring both receipts, the horizons are respectively ∞ and two. Both systems can be
represented by finite explicit controller tables and a fixed sound checker for the two proof packets.

Consequently we must distinguish a profile indexed by targets from a profile indexed by workloads,
joint receipt goals, or adaptive sequences of queries. This is relevant to an ATP whose investment in a
lemma or macro pays off across many targets. A vector of optimal costs for separate runs does not
certify that one strategy achieves all its coordinates simultaneously.

Definition 6.2 (Portfolio-aware potential). For a declared workload family U of terminal receipt
predicates on complete states, replace Gt by every GU , U ∈ U, in J . The componentwise order is an
exact comparison of the corresponding individual optimum values, not a guarantee that one common
policy attains them all. An ordered workload may be encoded by a task-progress coordinate in the
complete state.

Proposition 6.3 (Costed continuation transport). Suppose a map f : X → X ′ preserves goal
membership in the forward direction for each declared goal, and each source edge can be implemented by
a finite legal path from f (x) to f (y) costing at most w(e) and using at most r(e) revisions. These paths
must be executable in the image of each complete endpoint, not just in a matching projection. Then
Jk′ (f (s), G′U ) ≤ Jk (s, GU ) for all k, U .

Proof. Transport each finite source path by concatenating the edge implementations. The endpoint
is a goal by the observation condition, and both resources are bounded by the original sums. Taking
infima proves the result, including unreachable source goals. Empty source paths use the goal condition
directly.

   The hypothesis is a sufficient weighted forward refinement, not a necessary condition for numerical
dominance. If a setup transition to f (s) costs K and uses h revisions, the corresponding bound from its
source is K + Jk (s, GU ) with revision budget h + k, provided the target path remains legal after setup.
Composition of transport maps follows by path concatenation; setup costs add only when measured on
actual compatible successive states. This builds on the existing successor and revision quotient literature
[7, 9, 15]. It makes explicit why no comparison of abstract capabilities alone implies economically
favorable self-modification.


7     Discoverability: from possible machine to found machine
The original Picard competence–performance distinction now has an operational analogue. We distin-
guish:

    1. a semantically admissible transformation (the external conditions are true);

    2. a certifiably admissible transformation (an accepted finite witness exists);

    3. a discovered transformation (a particular process has actually produced its accepted witness);

    4. an installed and exercised transformation (the authorized legal events actually occur).

If the external gate is sound, (2) implies (1). If discovery and installation obey the contract, (4) implies
(3) implies (2) for that history. The converses are not automatic. A semantic graph is not generally
executable. One may compare separate graphs using the same costs on common edges; for a sound
restricted graph its optimum cannot beat the semantic optimum, assuming the same initial state and
goal observations. If the semantic graph’s costs omit certificate discovery, it is only an ideal comparator,
not the real charged value.

Theorem 7.1 (Effective certificate discovery, not effective omniscience). Suppose at each finitely
described parent, candidate revision–certificate pairs are effectively enumerable, checks terminate, and

Page 9

accepted pairs yield effectively computable legal successor descriptions (when authorization and execution
are included). Assume the analogous effective interfaces for ordinary transitions and goal recognition.
Then existence of a finite certified route to an accepted goal is recursively enumerable. With a computable
exhaustive finite successor list and known depth bound, existence is decidable. The latter claim extends
to a bounded rational cost test if all edge costs are computable exact rationals.

Proof. Dovetail enumeration of finite path descriptions and their finite checks, including parent matching
and execution permissions. Any genuine finite route is eventually enumerated and checked; accept
only a successful route. With finite complete successor lists and bounded depth, exhaustive traversal
terminates. Exact rational addition and comparison decide the cost condition. Neither construction
implies that traversal and replay together fit the same cost bound; that needs separate accounting.

Proposition 7.2 (A semantic barrier). Suppose the permitted contract language uniformly includes a
candidate ge with otherwise admissible finite operational effect and cumulative arithmetic increment
φe asserting that machine e never halts. Assume the consistent arithmetic baseline contains PA, has
a standard model compatible with the operational facts, and the other admissibility obligations are
independent of e. Then semantic admissibility of ge is equivalent to nonhalting. There is no sound
effective certificate gate that is complete for this family.

Proof. If e does not halt, the compatible standard model satisfies φe . If it halts, a finite computation
proves its halting in PA, so adjoining φe is inconsistent. A sound complete effective finite-witness gate
would enumerate precisely the nonhalting indices by dovetailing all witnesses, which is impossible. The
conclusion is conditional on this contract-language expressivity; it is not a claim about the admissibility
of any particular historical atom in GBG+S.

  This is an adaptation of standard halting/co-halting barriers and the author’s consistency-frontier
research, not a new undecidability result about arbitrary machines. A failed bounded search must be
reported as unresolved, not as proof of impossibility.

7.1    An exact discovery overhead in a restricted information model
Theorem 7.3 (Hidden revision query lower bound). Consider n ≥ 1 candidates with one hidden eligible
index i. The only information interface is a unit-cost query returning whether a specified candidate
is eligible. An accepted positive query is required for installation; no other observation identifies i.
Every eligible candidate has the same installation and subsequent proof charge a ≥ 0. Then the best
worst-case guaranteed total charge for deterministic discovery and execution is n + a. Under the
uniform hidden-index distribution, the best expected total charge for any always-correct randomized or
deterministic strategy is (n + 1)/2 + a.

Proof. Before a positive answer, all responses are negative. The sequence of distinct indices tried on
this transcript must eventually cover all indices, because each omitted index is a possible hidden state.
Repeating a query does not help. A deterministic strategy faces the index appearing last, requiring
n queries, and a permutation scan attains the bound. Under a uniform index, for each fixed random
seed of a correct strategy the first positive query has rank at least the position in its order of first
occurrences. The average of positions is (n + 1)/2; averaging over random seeds preserves the lower
bound. Scanning a fixed permutation attains it. Add the common execution charge a.

  With a supplied correct index, one positive query and execution cost 1 + a suffice. Thus the gap
between knowing an admissible gift and having to discover it is arbitrarily large even with a finite
candidate domain and constant per-candidate test cost. This elementary black-box bound does not
assert that real mathematical descriptions conceal their useful structure; they may expose exploitable

invariants. In particular it does not contradict receipt-guided revision selection in [10]. It motivates

8     A generalized Picard protocol
The original Picard Protocol certifies a recoded version of a machine. A stronger operational task is to
construct a nontrivially revised machine and certify an advantage while preserving the mathematical
contract. There is no need to claim that a machine proves its own unrestricted consistency. Instead the
protocol uses a parent-bound certificate with explicit external assumptions.
Definition 8.1 (Successor Picard packet). For a fixed parent s, workload U, and accounting convention,
a packet contains:
    1. a finite successor description s′ and a legal state-transfer implementation;

    2. a proof of scoped object-certificate preservation and a controller consistency witness satisfying
       Theorem 2.4;

    3. a proof that the intended successor is a sound ATP for its declared target domain, includ-
       ing completeness where promised; for an unbounded fair search this requires effective coverage,
       acceptance-complete checking and actual fair service;

    4. a separate certified comparison with a predeclared baseline under the same task, receipt semantics
       and accounting, including charged discovery, checking, installation, execution and recovery where
       required;

    5. an externally accepted parent-bound receipt, followed by a distinct authorized installation event
       and a recorded outcome if exercised.
A proposal packet that proves only items 1–3 is a certified candidate, not yet a demonstrated improvement.
An example that proves a route exists need not prove that the particular discovery policy finds it. No
part of the definition requires an autonomous installation.
Theorem 8.2 (Finite charged improvement criterion). Let L be a verified lower bound on the baseline’s
total charge for a specified workload, under its declared contract. If a legally executable discovery–
installation–settlement trace for a successor has certified upper charge U < L, with identical required
receipts, then its trace is strictly cheaper than every baseline trace covered by L. If only a baseline
measured or designated trace of charge L is given, the strict comparison is only with that trace. A
successor that admits the old strategy but incurs mandatory additional cost has no automatic net
advantage.
Proof. The fully charged successor trace costs at most U . Every baseline trace covered by the lower
bound costs at least L, so strict inequality gives the claim. The designated-trace version follows from
the same numerical comparison. Optionality of a strategy does not remove costs required to make that
strategy executable.

  For repeated independent matched tasks, if baseline cost is a, revised per-task cost is b and one-time
discovery/adoption cost is K, net improvement over m repetitions occurs exactly when m(a − b) > K,
assuming no other cost differences. This is the established amortization comparison used in CDF-C
and the successor work, not a new speedup theorem. It is essential to freeze caching, initial knowledge
and workload. An actual theorem prover is not improved merely because a research paper declares it
improved.

8.1   From existential paths to guaranteed play
If a state has adversarially or nondeterministically selected outcomes, replace the directed graph by
actions with nonempty finite outcome sets, each outcome carrying its cost. In a finite ranked complete
game whose rank strictly decreases on every nonterminal move, let V (x, k) be the least worst-case
charge for settling with at most k commits on every branch. At goal states set V = 0; at other terminals
set V = ∞. For legal actions with revision indicator r(a),

                          V (x, k) = min          max      {w + V (y, k − r(a))}.
                                     a:r(a)≤k (y,w)∈Out(x,a)

A minimum over no actions is infinity. Backward induction on rank establishes the recurrence by the
first-action decomposition. A selected minimizing action at every reachable decision boundary supplies
a winning policy if the value is finite. For a certified upper bound every possible continuation of that
policy must be covered; for a losing-set witness every allowed action must have at least one permitted
successor in the losing region of the budget-expanded game. This is the ranked game construction of [5]
with an extra revision-count coordinate. It is not valid to replace the maximum with a favorable single
successor or to apply the acyclic recurrence without addressing cycles, nontermination and fairness.
Hidden states require belief-state strategies rather than access to their true values.


9     The 24th-century specialization
In the author’s terminology a 24th-century machine operates in an underlying sovereign game. The
sovereign rules determine which gifts can be admitted and exercised. The results above require only
a legal complete-state transition model. Thus the specialization is to make the controller’s revision
edges correspond to admitted and actually exercised gifts, retaining the exact object-theory proof task.
The stage-indexed controller consistency proof is not a substitute for an interpretation of all gifts in
GBG+S.
   The audited sovereign manual [2] supplies a typed, cumulative admission discipline and a restricted
structural existence construction; it does not establish unrestricted syntactic relative consistency. For a
faithful implementation, a candidate’s support-indexed grammar, current parent, cumulative increments,
observable novelty, identity and authorization, STOP status, activation, and actual legal player move
must satisfy that rulebook. The abstract depth-d chains of Theorem 4.2 must not be called actual
GBG+S witnesses until all those obligations have been discharged. The count k in this paper counts
commits, not the number of atom names; repeated atoms inside a gift do not change its distinct-support
count. A finite vocabulary does not automatically give an effectively exhaustive finite expression
catalogue. CDF-C has its own independent contract and clock/credit restrictions. A legal permission
never implies compulsory installation.

12     Open problems and conclusion
Future-sufficient potential. Characterize the weakest effectively checkable equivalence on complete
machine states that preserves a specified family of future revision-budgeted workload values and admits
legal lifting of actions. Equality of singleton values is insufficient. Strong behavioral refinements are
sufficient but may be much finer than needed. Compare with W2’s existing stronger structural quotient,
rather than rediscovering it.
   Obstruction-guided discovery. Given a certified bound on settlement in the current controller,
find a nontrivial family where structural obstruction information yields a provably cheaper search for
admissible revisions than an equal-information baseline. The black-box query bound identifies a regime
in which merely possessing a candidate grammar is not enough. The aim is a theorem or a controlled
experiment about finding useful revisions, not a known gift inserted by hand.
   Successor incomparability and strength. Extend Theorem 5.1 to costed, fully charged imple-
mentations or explicit proof-size families, with independently justified lower bounds. Study when a
better workload profile admits an efficient simulation in a richer state space, and when it cannot. The
existing result is finite and stage-based, not asymptotic.
   Discoverable depth. Study families where the cheapest semantic route, cheapest finitely certified
route, and cheapest route achievable by a fixed discovery process are different. Establish the appropriate
effective representation and information model before claiming a gap. The present arithmetic barrier
depends on contract expressivity, and the finite query theorem depends on hidden-index access.
   Practical next experiment. Freeze an ATP calculus, a held-out target workload and a baseline
search policy. Compare (i) frozen search, (ii) fixed catalogue of conservative checked macros, (iii)
obstruction-guided proposal and external revision admission. Charge failed proposals, certificate
construction and validation, migration, proof expansion, search and recovery. Record full receipt success,
uncertainty and costs; do not infer empirical benefit from the abstract witness. No such deployment
was performed in preparing this paper.
   The original Depths of a Simulation defined machines and compared their computational behaviors.
