    A Mathematical Framework for Multi-Agent Conjecture Settling



                                                Abstract
          We describe an abstract framework for mathematical conjecture settling by a collection of
      cooperating agents operating over a shared bank of verified mathematical statements.
          Agents may pursue different settlement objectives, including proving a conjecture, proving
      its negation, or establishing its independence from a specified axiom system.
          Unlike independent dovetailed proof searches, the agents communicate through a monotoni-
      cally growing shared lemma bank. Every verified result produced by one agent therefore becomes
      available as a premise to every subsequent agent.
          The framework separates the mathematical structure of conjecture settling from any par-
      ticular implementation by automated theorem provers, proof assistants, search algorithms, or
      learned systems.


1    Formal Setting
Let A be a formal axiom system and let C be a conjecture expressed in the language of A.
   We distinguish three possible settlement objectives:

                                                 A ⊢ C,


                                                A ⊢ ¬C,
    and

                                      A⊬C        and       A ⊬ ¬C.
   The third condition corresponds to independence of C from A, assuming the relevant consistency
and metatheoretic conditions are satisfied.
   Failure to discover a proof of either C or ¬C is not by itself a certificate of independence.


2    The Shared Lemma Bank
Let Lt denote the collection of verified statements available after round t.
   Initially,

                                                L0 = A.
    More generally, one may distinguish background axioms B from problem-specific assumptions
A, in which case

                                              L0 = B ∪ A.

   During round t, agents may derive new verified statements. Let Dt denote the set of statements
successfully deposited during that round.
   The bank evolves according to

                                          Lt+1 = Lt ∪ Dt .
    Consequently,

                                             Lt ⊆ Lt+1 .
   Thus mathematical knowledge accumulated by the system is monotonic: once a verified lemma
has entered the bank, it remains available to all future agents.


3     Agents
For the initial system, consider three agents:

                                           G = {P, R, I}.
    Their objectives are different even though they operate over the same bank.

3.1   The Prover
The prover P seeks a certificate establishing

                                                 A ⊢ C.
    At each activation, P may select any finite collection of statements from the current bank,

                                            ΓP ⊆ﬁn Lt ,
    apply an admissible inference procedure, and attempt to produce a new verified statement.

3.2   The Refuter
The refuter R seeks a certificate establishing

                                                 A ⊢ ¬C.
   It has access to the same lemma bank as the prover.
   Thus a lemma discovered while attempting to prove C may later assist the search for ¬C, and
conversely.

3.3   The Independence Agent
The independence agent I seeks a certificate establishing that C is independent of A.
   Conceptually, its objective is

                                    A⊬C           and      A ⊬ ¬C.
    Unlike ordinary proof search, this generally requires metamathematical certificates.
    For example, an independence argument may establish suitable models

                                             M |= A ∪ {C}
    and

                                           N |= A ∪ {¬C}.
   Other implementations may use relative consistency proofs, forcing, model construction, proof-
theoretic methods, or other appropriate metamathematical techniques.


4    One-Step Agent Action
An agent activation is deliberately abstract.
   For an agent g, define

                                                Stepg (Lt )
    to be a computation which reads any finite subset of the current bank and returns either no
result or a single candidate contribution.
    Thus

                                        Stepg (Lt ) ∈ {⊥} ∪ F ,
   where F is the set of well-formed statements or certificates and ⊥ denotes failure to produce a
contribution on that activation.
   If the returned contribution passes the system’s verifier, it is deposited into the bank.
   Therefore an agent follows the abstract cycle

                          read bank → take one step → verify → deposit .
   The agent may use arbitrarily many previously established premises in taking that step, while
depositing at most one new verified result per activation.


5    Global State
Let St denote the complete state of the conjecture-settling system at round t.
   Abstractly,

                                    St = (Lt , sP (t), sR (t), sI (t)) ,
    where sP , sR , sI represent any private search state maintained by the respective agents.
    A transition operator

                                            T : St −→ St+1
    represents one scheduled activation.
    For a round-robin scheduler, for example,

                                          P, R, I, P, R, I, . . .
  are activated successively.
  The mathematical framework does not require round-robin scheduling. Any fair scheduling rule
may be substituted.

6      Settlement Certificates
Define three certificate classes:

                                 Cert+ (C),         Cert− (C),       Cert0 (C).
      They represent respectively:

                                            Cert+ (C) :     A ⊢ C,


                                           Cert− (C) :      A ⊢ ¬C,
      and

                                    Cert0 (C) :    C is independent of A.
      A conjecture is settled when the bank contains a valid member of one of these certificate classes.


7      The Conjecture-Settling Procedure
We may therefore define a partial conjecture-settling function

                                                    CSA (C).
      Its possible outputs are

                        CSA (C) ∈ {PROVED, REFUTED, INDEPENDENT}.
      More precisely,

                                           CSA (C) = PROVED
      when a valid certificate for A ⊢ C is deposited;

                                          CSA (C) = REFUTED
      when a valid certificate for A ⊢ ¬C is deposited; and

                                      CSA (C) = INDEPENDENT
      when a valid independence certificate is deposited.
      The function is partial because an unrestricted mathematical search need not terminate.


8      Stopping Rule
Let

                                                  Solved(Lt , C)
      mean that Lt contains a valid settlement certificate for C.
      The system terminates at the first time

                                    τ = min{t : Solved(Lt , C)}.
   At time τ , all agents halt.
   Thus the agents compete with respect to settlement objectives while cooperating with respect
to mathematical knowledge.


9    Monotonicity
Proposition 1. If statements are added to the bank but never removed, then
                                              Lt ⊆ Lt+1
for every t.
Proof. By definition,
                                          Lt+1 = Lt ∪ Dt .
Therefore every element of Lt is also an element of Lt+1 .


10 Soundness of the Bank
Let V be a trusted verification procedure.
   A candidate statement x may enter the bank only when

                                              V (x) = 1.
    Consequently, search and verification are separated.
    An agent may be heuristic, stochastic, learned, incomplete, or even frequently wrong in its
proposals without compromising the logical integrity of the shared bank, provided that the verifier
itself is sound.
Proposition 2. Suppose every initial element of L0 is admissible and every subsequent deposit is
accepted only after sound verification. Then every element of every Lt is admissible.


11 Generalization to Many Agents
Nothing essential depends on there being exactly three agents.
   Let

                                       G = {g1 , g2 , . . . , gn }.
    Each agent has some objective Oi and transition procedure

                                                Stepgi .
    All agents communicate through the same evolving mathematical memory:

                                       L0 ⊆ L1 ⊆ L2 ⊆ · · · .
   Different agents may employ entirely different reasoning mechanisms.
   For example, one agent could be implemented by a saturation-based automated theorem prover,
another by model search, another by an SMT solver, and another by a learned proof-search policy.
   The shared-bank abstraction does not depend upon their internal implementations.

12 Example: Law of Excluded Middle
As an initial test conjecture, consider

                                           C := φ ∨ ¬φ.
   The prover seeks

                                           A ⊢ φ ∨ ¬φ.
   The refuter seeks

                                          A ⊢ ¬(φ ∨ ¬φ).
   The independence agent seeks an appropriate certificate that neither statement follows from
the chosen axiom system.
   The expected outcome depends critically upon the underlying logic.
   For example, the law of excluded middle is valid in classical propositional logic but is not
generally derivable in intuitionistic logic.
   This illustrates why every conjecture-settling problem must specify not merely a conjecture C,
but the formal system A relative to which settlement is sought.


13 Discussion
The essential feature of the framework is not parallelism alone.
    Independent proof searches can be dovetailed without communicating. Here, however, successful
intermediate mathematical work becomes shared infrastructure.
    If agent gi proves lemma λ, then

                                            λ ∈ Lt+1 ,
    and every later agent may immediately use λ.
    The system therefore combines competition among objectives with cooperation among deriva-
tions.
    A useful conceptual summary is

                many searches + one verified memory + certificate-based stopping .


14 Conclusion
We have defined an abstract multi-agent conjecture-settling process consisting of:

  1. a formal background theory,

  2. a conjecture,

  3. a monotonically growing shared lemma bank,

  4. multiple agents pursuing distinct settlement objectives,

  5. a trusted verification mechanism,

                                                6

<<<PAGE_BREAK>>>
  6. a scheduler, and

  7. a certificate-based stopping condition.

   The framework deliberately leaves the internal reasoning algorithm of each agent unspecified.
   This permits automated theorem provers, model finders, proof assistants, symbolic algorithms,
and learned reasoning systems to be substituted without changing the abstract conjecture-settling
architecture.
